A post climbed to the top of r/ClaudeAI this week with a blunt title: [I am done with this shit](https://www.reddit.com/r/ClaudeAI/comments/1wm5c21/i_am_done_with_this_shit/). It reposted a rant from X. Half a month into a new role at a big company, the author found that the specs, code, tests, PRDs, tickets, ticket resolutions, and reports were all produced by Claude Code. Everyone from L1 to L7 did the same thing all day: talk to Claude. People worked 12 to 13 hours a day, mostly pressing Enter. Management kept asking one question: "Pushing code is not the bottleneck anymore, so why are we slow?"

The comments quickly passed 800. The most ironic part was the TL;DR generated by the moderator bot. An AI summarized a crowd's complaints about AI, and its verdict was harsher than most of the comments: you are no longer an engineer, just an "organic vessel designed to press enter," and your only real use is to be the living person who can be blamed when the AI breaks something.

<!-- more -->

The post itself cannot be verified. It came from an anonymous account, names no company, and some commenters suspected the text itself was written by AI. What interests me more is the comment section: hundreds of people recognizing themselves and describing what is happening at their own companies.

## 800 Comments Describe the Same Workplace

The top comments describe strikingly similar scenes.

One described how requirements move: my AI parses my coworker's AI-written requirements doc, his AI reads my tweaks and passes them back for my AI to redo, and nobody talks to each other anymore. A consultant put it more bluntly: I use AI to write the report, my manager uses AI to review it, the client uses AI to read it, a closed loop where nobody actually does anything.

Review is where it hurts most. One commenter said the team receives 50K lines of PRs in a single day that must be reviewed, tested, and checked against what the customer asked for. A real review would take at least 10 days, and the manager's answer was "use AI to review it." Another said tasks that used to get 3 days now have to ship in 2 to 3 hours, with no time to test, so they wrote another set of Agents to review and test the code, sometimes using the same model that wrote it. A senior developer who has been on the same project for 15 years was reprimanded for spending time cleaning up AI code and pushing for better abstractions and shared libraries, and was told to "lower my standards" because coding is Claude's job now. He added one line: the project has more bugs than ever.

One comment captured the whole chain. My manager pretends he understood everything, my senior pretends he understood everything, and I pretend I grasped the AI-generated code in an hour. Nobody knows anything. When something breaks, we say "I may have made a mistake," when the mistake was really made by the tool.

The line that drew the most emotional response was from the original post: "There is no sense of victory." One reply said that no matter how good the result is, there is no satisfaction, because all I did was prompt, press Enter, and half-check it.

## Plenty of People Are Doing Fine

The thread was not all despair. The other half had a different response: switch companies.

One person at a startup said all their documents and code are written by AI too, but every single thing has a responsible person who must be able to explain what was done. Another team's rule is stricter: if you open the PR, you are 100% responsible for it. You are expected to understand what it does, and if you don't, you go back to the drawing board. They also deliberately stopped letting oversized PRs through. A senior IC who owns the entire stack at a small finance firm said he has set Claude up with guardrails, testing, and verification, and now sleeps better and works his contracted hours. A veteran with nearly 30 years in the industry said he has never felt this excited.

Both sides use the same class of tools. The difference comes down to one variable: **whether someone who actually understands the generated work is ultimately responsible for it.** The teams doing well treat understanding as part of delivery. The teams falling apart treat it as a cost to cut. One comment called AI an amplifier that magnifies bad management first, and I agree.

## Code Is Not the Bottleneck, So Everything Slowed Down

Management's premise is correct. Writing code is no longer the bottleneck. The conclusion drawn from it is wrong.

Anyone who has studied the theory of constraints knows a bottleneck does not disappear. It moves. Code generation got ten times faster, while the speed of understanding code, verifying it, and judging whether it matches intent did not change. Pushing harder on the non-bottleneck stage only piles output in front of the bottleneck as work-in-progress inventory. One commenter said it in a sentence: writing code isn't the bottleneck, reading and evaluating it is.

Code nobody has read is not an asset. It is inventory with an unknown defect rate. The bill does not arrive on the day of the merge. It comes due all at once on the day of the incident. One commenter put it coldly: good luck troubleshooting outages caused by code no one even remembers seeing.

I have hit this myself. In {% post_link fast-is-the-most-expensive-slow.en 'Fast Is the Most Expensive Slow' %}, I let Claude follow a beautiful ROADMAP all the way to M7. CI was green and coverage hit the target. When I installed it, voice did not work, the persona definition was never used, and the UI was not even packaged into the installer. What saved the project was a two-hour audit. The company in the Reddit post has removed exactly those two hours from the process.

## Why AI Reviewing AI Cannot Catch It

"Use AI to review it" sounds like a reasonable patch. One more gate is better than none. The problem is that a gate gets its value from independence: the ways it fails must not be correlated with the thing it checks.

When the same model writes the code, the review, and the tests, all three share the same context, the same wrong assumption, and the same blind spots. Tests written from the implementation simply encode the implementation a second time. The review bot leaves comments, the author feeds them back to Claude to fix, and the loop never touches a single external fact. It looks like three gates. In practice there is one point of view.

In {% post_link what-engineers-are-still-for.en 'What Are Engineers Still For?' %}, I argued that the gates that let an Agent run overnight must be anchored in real samples, rerunnable metrics, and counter-metrics that block the cheating paths. The gates described in this thread are the opposite: opinions from the same source. **A review with no external facts in it is just a second pass of generation.**

## Humans Are Kept Around to Take the Blame

One comment was quoted again and again: technically, you're an organic vessel designed to be held accountable for what Claude is doing, because Claude can't be held accountable.

Madeleine Clare Elish, who studied accidents in automated systems, called this position a moral crumple zone: like the crumple zone of a car, the human in an automated system has little control but absorbs the impact. Cory Doctorow calls it a reverse centaur, a person serving as an appendage to the machine. Applied to this thread, both mean the same thing. The human stays in the loop so that someone can sign off and be held accountable when things go wrong. Their judgment is optional.

The trouble is that responsibility presupposes control. A person with no time to read the code and no authority to slow down can only put a signature on the outcome. That is not control. The more dangerous effect is an organizational illusion. Management believes every piece of code has a human checkpoint, and the status report says human-in-the-loop, when in reality there is only a string of Enter presses. A fully automated system at least knows that no one reviews it, which forces the team to build gates. **A human step that does not read the code only convinces everyone they are already safe.**

## This Does Not Contradict What I Wrote Before

In the same article I made another claim: tools like `/goal` will take the Enter key out of human hands, and most implementation-focused engineers will be replaced. Someone could say this thread proves it.

I think it shows the opposite configuration. With `/goal`, the human leaves the Enter key to define measurable goals, build the harness, and watch whether the metrics are being gamed. The machine can run through the night because gates stop it when it drifts. The company in this thread nails its people to the Enter key and has no one defining gates. It gets neither side: no tireless machine loop and no human judgment.

Pressing Enter is exactly the step that gets automated first. Part of why those 12-hour days feel empty is that everyone vaguely knows they are occupying a seat that will eventually be deleted.

So the comments saying "you just don't know how to use AI yet" are only half right. Individual skill cannot rescue an organization that allows no time for understanding. For the individual, though, one reply offered a practical direction: become the person who does know what is going on, then ask for a raise.

## Where the Sense of Victory Went

Back to that line: "There is no sense of victory."

The commenters who still felt pride had something in common. A solo game developer said he doesn't read much of the code anymore, but he points the AI at how to resolve the bugs it causes and how to improve what it is doing for the user. Another built a natural-language Windows scripting tool. He designed the classes, made sure it had proper logging, and documented every bug they fixed and why it occurred: "AI did most of the coding but the app is how I want it because I told AI to make it that way."

A sense of victory comes from a judgment that reality confirms: I think the problem is here, I decide to do it this way, and the result proves me right. Pressing Enter involves no judgment, so there is nothing that can be proven right. In {% post_link who-holds-the-reins.en 'Who Holds the Reins?' %}, I wrote that the What is the reins and the How is the horsepower. People who hand over the What get nothing but the feeling of speed.

Back to management's question: if writing code is no longer the bottleneck, why are we slow? Because the only bottleneck left is understanding, and you have forbidden anyone to spend time there. At 3 a.m., during the next production outage, the engineer who gets paged will most likely open Claude first and ask it what this code does.
